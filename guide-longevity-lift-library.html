Section 01

Why You Should Lift for Life

I'm not interested in your one-rep max. I'm interested in whether you can get off the floor at 80, carry your own groceries, and take the stairs without thinking about it. That's what this guide trains — the everyday strength that decides how independent you stay as you age.

01
Strength Is the Lever You Control

Muscle mass and strength fall as you age. The good news: they respond to training at every age. Of everything that shapes how you grow older, strength is the piece most in your hands.

02
Train Function, Not the Mirror

The things that predict aging well aren't aesthetic — they're functional: leg strength, grip, balance, how fast you walk. This guide trains the movement patterns you actually use, not muscles in isolation.

03
Earn the Range, Then Load It

Own a clean rep through a full range before you add weight. Load a good pattern and each lift makes you more durable. Load a bad one and you just reinforce the habits that get people hurt.

A Menu, Not a Prescription

You don't need all ten lifts every session. Think of this as a menu built around the six movements your body relies on — squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull, and carry. Hit each one across your week and you've covered your whole body. From there, pick the lifts and the levels that fit your equipment, your joints, and where you are today.

How to Use This Guide
1

Train these patterns 2–4× a week. Pick lifts that cover all six.

2

Use a load you can control with clean form. Aim for 2–4 sets of 6–12 reps, slow and controlled, unless a lift says otherwise.

3

Start where you are. Every lift has an easier and a harder version. Own the one you can do today, then build from there.

Lower Body · Squat Pattern

Squat

Goblet · Front · Back
QuadsGlutesCoreAnkles

How to Perform It

  1. 1Set your feet shoulder-width with toes turned out 10–20°. Screw your feet into the floor to build tension through the hips.
  2. 2Drive your knees out over your toes and descend until your hip crease drops below your knee, heels glued to the floor.
  3. 3Stand by pushing the floor away and squeezing your glutes at the top. Control the descent — never just drop.
The Coaching Cue

Knees track over toes, heels stay down, and spine stays in a neutral position. Depth comes from the hips and ankles — not from rounding the low back.

Common Mistake

Heels lifting or knees caving inward. Both usually trace back to stiff ankles or weak glutes — fix the mobility, don’t just force the depth.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Every time you stand up from a chair or off the floor, you're squatting. It's one of the most repeated movements in your day, and in aging research, how easily someone rises from a seated position is one of the better predictors of staying independent. Keep the pattern strong now and you keep it for decades.

Scale It to You

Easier
Box / Chair Squat
2–3 × 8–10

Squat down to lightly touch a bench or chair, then stand. Grooves the pattern and builds confidence in the bottom.

The Standard
Goblet Squat
3 × 8–12

Hold a weight at the chest; the counterbalance lets you sit deeper with control. The most joint-friendly place to start loading.

Harder
Front / Back Squat
3–4 × 5–8

Load on the front rack or the back for serious, progressive lower-body strength once the pattern is clean.

Lower Body · Hinge Pattern

Deadlift

Trap Bar · Kettlebell · Barbell
GlutesHamstringsBackGrip

How to Perform It

  1. 1Stand with the load over your mid-foot, feet hip-width. Hinge by pushing your hips back with soft knees.
  2. 2Take your grip, then pull the slack out of the bar and set a flat, neutral spine — chest up, lats tight.
  3. 3Brace hard, then drive the floor away with your legs, keeping the load close to your body the whole way up.
  4. 4Finish tall with the glutes squeezed — no leaning back. Reverse the exact same path to lower under control.
The Coaching Cue

The bar travels in a straight vertical line, close to your shins and thighs. Power comes from the legs and hips while the spine stays neutral and braced.

Common Mistake

Rounding the low back or yanking the weight up. The back should transmit force, not generate the lift — let the hips and legs do the work.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Every time you pick something up off the ground — a suitcase, a grandkid, a bag of soil — you're hinging. It's also the pattern people get hurt on most, usually because they round the back under load. Train the hinge well and you build the exact strength that keeps lifting from becoming the thing you're afraid of.

Scale It to You

Easier
Elevated / KB Deadlift
3 × 8

Raise the load on blocks or use a kettlebell to shorten the range while you build the hinge.

The Standard
Trap-Bar Deadlift
3 × 6–8

The most spine-friendly entry point — the load sits at your sides, keeping you more upright.

Harder
Barbell / Romanian DL
3–4 × 5–6

Full range from the floor, or a loaded hinge that hits the hamstrings and glutes.

Lower Body · Single-Leg

Lunge

Reverse · Forward · Walking
QuadsGlutesHip StabilizersBalance

How to Perform It

  1. 1Stand tall. Step one foot back into a long, stable stride (the reverse lunge).
  2. 2Lower straight down until both knees reach about 90° — front shin vertical, back knee hovering just off the floor.
  3. 3Keep your torso upright and your weight through the front heel and mid-foot.
  4. 4Drive through the front leg to return to standing. Complete all reps, then switch sides.
The Coaching Cue

The front knee tracks over the 2nd and 3rd toes, hips stay square, torso stays tall. The descent is straight down — not pitching forward onto the knee.

Common Mistake

Letting the front knee cave inward or the torso fall forward. Shorten the range until you can own it with control before adding depth or load.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Stairs, curbs, catching yourself on uneven ground — that's all single-leg strength. The lunge also tends to expose the left-to-right differences most people don't know they have, which is often where a future hip or knee problem starts. Find the weaker side and you can train it back up.

Scale It to You

Easier
Static Split Squat
2–3 × 8

Feet planted in a stride, just travel up and down. Hold a support if you need it.

The Standard
Reverse Lunge
3 × 8 / side

Stepping back is easier on the knees than stepping forward — the best default for people who have knee pain.

Harder
Walking / Loaded Lunge
3 × 10 / side

Add dumbbells or travel through space to load the pattern and challenge balance.

Lower Body · Frontal Plane

Lateral Lunge

Bodyweight · Goblet · Slider
AdductorsGlutesHip Mobility

How to Perform It

  1. 1Stand tall with feet together. Take a wide step directly out to one side.
  2. 2Sit back and down into the stepping-side hip, bending that knee while the other leg stays long and straight.
  3. 3Keep both feet flat with toes pointing forward as the hips push back.
  4. 4Push off the bent leg to return to the start. Move slowly and with control throughout.
The Coaching Cue

Sit into the hip of the bent leg with the knee tracking over the 2nd–3rd toes. The trailing leg stays long.

Common Mistake

The bent knee caving inward, or rushing the rep. Keep it slow — the frontal plane is where most people are weakest and least practiced.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Catching a slip, stepping out of the way, getting in and out of a car — those are side-to-side demands, and most training ignores them completely. Almost everything we do in the gym happens straight ahead. Train the frontal plane and your hips are ready for the directions real life actually moves.

Scale It to You

Easier
Assisted Lateral Lunge
2 × 8 / side

Hold a support and work a partial, comfortable depth while you build hip mobility.

The Standard
Bodyweight Lateral Lunge
3 × 8 / side

Slow and controlled to full depth, knee tracking cleanly over the toes.

Harder
Goblet Lateral Lunge
3 × 10 / side

Hold a weight at the chest to load the adductors and glutes through range.

Upper Body · Horizontal Push

Horizontal Press

Push-Up · Bench · Dumbbell
ChestShouldersTricepsCore

How to Perform It

  1. 1Set hands slightly wider than your shoulders, body in one straight line from head to heels.
  2. 2Brace your core and glutes so the hips don’t sag. Pull your shoulder blades back and down.
  3. 3Lower with control until your chest is just above the floor, elbows at about 45° to your ribs.
  4. 4Press the floor away to a full lockout, holding the rigid plank line the entire time.
The Coaching Cue

A rigid, straight-line body with elbows tucked to roughly 45°. Move through the full range with control — chest to just above the floor, then full lockout.

Common Mistake

Sagging hips, flaring elbows, or bouncing the bar off the chest. Own the full range under control instead of chasing reps with half-depth.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Pushing yourself up off the floor or out of a low chair is horizontal pressing strength. So is getting back up after a fall. Keep that strength and a stumble stays a stumble — something you get up from on your own.

Scale It to You

Easier
Incline Push-Up
3 × 8–12

Hands on a bench, counter, or wall to reduce the load while keeping perfect form.

The Standard
Floor Push-Up
3 × 8–15

Full range, rigid body, elbows tucked. The most accessible pressing strength builder there is.

Harder
Bench / DB Press
3–4 × 6–10

Load it for progressive pressing strength once bodyweight reps are easy.

Upper Body · Vertical Push

Overhead Press

Dumbbell · Barbell · Landmine
ShouldersUpper BackTricepsCore

How to Perform It

  1. 1Stand tall with the weights at shoulder height, elbows slightly in front of the body.
  2. 2Brace your core and squeeze your glutes so you don’t arch the low back.
  3. 3Press straight overhead, moving your head slightly back and then through as the weights pass your face.
  4. 4Finish with arms locked out and biceps near your ears. Lower under control back to the shoulders.
The Coaching Cue

Ribs down, glutes tight, and a straight bar path that finishes with the weight stacked over your mid-foot. The motion comes from the shoulders, not a low-back arch.

Common Mistake

Arching the low back to heave the weight up. If you can’t press it without leaning back, the load is too heavy or the shoulder mobility isn’t there yet.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Overhead reach is one of the first things a desk-bound, rounded-forward lifestyle takes from you. Pressing keeps your shoulders both strong and mobile, so reaching the top shelf or the overhead bin stays easy instead of becoming a chore.

Scale It to You

Easier
Landmine / ½-Kneel Press
3 × 8

A more forgiving angle for stiff shoulders, and the half-kneel removes the low-back cheat.

The Standard
DB Overhead Press
3 × 8–10

Independent arms even out left-to-right imbalances — a great default for most people.

Harder
Barbell Overhead Press
3–4 × 5–6

The heaviest, most full-body pressing strength once the pattern is owned.

Upper Body · Vertical Pull

Pull-Up

Assisted · Pulldown · Full
LatsBicepsUpper BackGrip

How to Perform It

  1. 1Grip the bar slightly wider than your shoulders, palms forward. Start from a full hang with active shoulders.
  2. 2Set your shoulder blades down and back — think about pulling them into your back pockets.
  3. 3Drive your elbows down toward your ribs and pull your chest up toward the bar.
  4. 4Lower all the way to a full, controlled hang. No swinging or kipping for momentum.
The Coaching Cue

Lead with the shoulder blades, then the elbows — chest up to the bar, full hang at the bottom. Smooth and controlled, never swinging for momentum.

Common Mistake

Half-range reps and a dead, passive hang. Pull all the way up and control all the way down — assisted versions count just as much.

Why It Matters for Longevity

The lats attach all the way down at your lower back, so pulling strength supports both your shoulders and your spine. A strong pull is also what lets you haul your own bodyweight up — out of a pool, over a wall, off the ground.

Scale It to You

Easier
Lat Pulldown
3 × 10–12

Dial the load to exactly your level and groove the pulling pattern.

The Standard
Assisted Pull-Up
3 × 6–10

Band- or machine-assisted bodyweight pulls that build toward the real thing.

Harder
Full Pull-Up
3–4 × 5–8

Strict reps from a dead hang; add weight once they’re easy.

Upper Body · Horizontal Pull

Row

Cable · Dumbbell · Barbell
Upper BackLatsBicepsRear Delts

How to Perform It

  1. 1Hinge to a flat-back position, or sit tall at a cable. Let the weight hang with the shoulder blade reaching forward.
  2. 2Start the pull by drawing the shoulder blade back, then pull the elbow toward your hip.
  3. 3Bring the handle to your lower ribs or upper belly, squeezing the mid-back hard.
  4. 4Lower with control to a full stretch. Keep the torso still — no jerking or swinging.
The Coaching Cue

Pull with the back, not the arm — shoulder blade first, elbow to the hip, full squeeze. A complete range of motion beats a heavier weight.

Common Mistake

Heaving the weight with body english and a short range. Slow it down, feel the mid-back work, and let the upper back — not momentum — move the load.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Your upper back is what holds you upright against gravity and a long day at a desk. Strengthen it and you've got a counter to the rounded, forward-head position that creeps in over years.

Scale It to You

Easier
Supported Cable / Chest Row
3 × 10–12

Torso braced against a pad, removing any low-back demand so you can focus on the squeeze.

The Standard
One-Arm DB Row
3 × 8–12 / side

Full range, one side at a time, to even out imbalances.

Harder
Barbell Row
3–4 × 6–8

Heavier bilateral pulling that builds serious upper-back strength.

Posterior Chain · Bridge

Hip Thrust

Glute Bridge · Hip Thrust · Single-Leg
GlutesHamstringsPosterior Chain

How to Perform It

  1. 1Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width with heels near your glutes.
  2. 2Tuck your ribs and gently tilt the pelvis to flatten your low back.
  3. 3Drive through your heels and squeeze your glutes to lift your hips into a straight line from knees to shoulders.
  4. 4Pause and squeeze at the top, then lower with control. Move the hips and ribs together as one unit.
The Coaching Cue

Move the hips and ribs as a single unit and finish with a hard glute squeeze — a straight line from knees to shoulders. The glutes lift you, not the low back.

Common Mistake

Over-arching and lifting with the lower back instead of the glutes. Keep the ribs down and the spine neutral so the glutes do the work they’re built for.

Why It Matters for Longevity

When the glutes are weak, the lower back picks up work it wasn't built for — and that's a common driver of the back pain I see in the clinic. Strong glutes drive your walking, your stairs, and every hinge, and they take load off the spine.

Scale It to You

Easier
Bodyweight Glute Bridge
3 × 12–15

Floor-based — master the squeeze and the neutral-spine position first.

The Standard
Barbell / DB Hip Thrust
3 × 8–12

Upper back on a bench, load across the hips, for the strongest glute training there is.

Harder
Single-Leg Hip Thrust
3 × 8 / side

One leg at a time exposes and corrects left-to-right asymmetry.

Total Body · Loaded Carry

Loaded Carry

Suitcase · Farmer · Mixed
GripCoreTrunk StabilityTraps

How to Perform It

  1. 1Pick up a heavy weight in one hand (suitcase) or both (farmer).
  2. 2Stand tall: ribs down, shoulders back, chest proud, eyes ahead.
  3. 3Brace your core — especially on the suitcase carry, where one side is fighting to tip you over.
  4. 4Walk slow, smooth, controlled steps for the prescribed time or distance, then set the weight down with a hinge.
The Coaching Cue

Walk tall and slow with ribs stacked over hips — no leaning, no rushing. The core stays braced to resist the load pulling you to one side.

Common Mistake

Hurrying, hunching, or letting the loaded side pull you into a lean. Slow down — the value is in resisting the tilt with a tall, braced trunk.

Why It Matters for Longevity

Grip strength is one of the most reliable markers in all of aging research — a stand-in for whole-body strength that tracks with both function and how long people live. And hauling groceries, luggage, and kids around is just a loaded carry you're already doing without calling it one.

Scale It to You

Easier
Short Suitcase Carry
3 trips · 20–30s

A lighter load in one hand for shorter bouts while you build grip and trunk stability.

The Standard
Suitcase / Farmer Carry
3 trips · 40–60s

Tall and steady for time per side. The everyday-strength staple.

Harder
Heavy Farmer Carry
3–4 trips

Build toward roughly half your bodyweight split between both hands.

The Progressive Overload Reminder

Add a little each week — a rep, a few pounds, a touch more depth. Your tissues adapt to demand, but only when that demand keeps climbing. When a lift starts to feel easy, that's your cue to make it harder.
